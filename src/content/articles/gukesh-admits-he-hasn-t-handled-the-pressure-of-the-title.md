Six days before his first title defense, world champion Gukesh Dommaraju has said plainly that the job has been getting the better of him. "I think clearly so far I've not been able to handle it in the best possible way," he said, in a dinner conversation with Viswanathan Anand and ChessBase India's Sagar Shah.

[Chess.com released the 86-minute video last week](https://www.chess.com/news/view/gukesh-pressure-sindarov-world-championship), filmed at Anand's home in Chennai.

The admission is notable mostly for its bluntness. Champions heading into a match usually project calm, and Gukesh instead listed the damage.

**A year of poor results**

The 20-year-old has fallen to 28th in the world at 2710. He scored 3.5/9 at the Prague Masters in March and 3/10 at Norway Chess in June. In July he managed 2/7 at the Quantbox Chennai Grand Masters, which cost him another 14 rating points.

He finished last or tied for last in all three events. His 8.5/11 at the 46th FIDE Chess Olympiad helped India to silver, which was some relief, but Gukesh himself says there have been only "maybe two or three tournaments in the last one year that I've been happy about."

He pointed to missed chances in Chennai and positions he would normally convert. In his reading, the trouble was not only chess. "There is something with a lot of expectations, a lot of pressure that I'm dealing with and I've not found the right way to maybe handle it."

**Smiling as a method**

His remedy is modest. "I'm trying to be happy every day, smile every day," he said, adding that he is working hard but also doing things he enjoys away from chess.

On criticism, he said he spent a long time trying to avoid it, though "you know that people are saying things." A friend suggested he use his love of comedy and play along with hecklers instead.

Anand, who has had his own bad stretches, backed the approach as "basically the correct attitude."

His advice was to drop the idea of recovering something. "The day you start to come back is when you stop expecting things. You just try to play a good game of chess."

He added that the best chess comes when a player returns to a natural state and thinks about moves, not about everyone else's expectations. Even so, he conceded that "somewhere deep down you can't push the thought completely away."

**What Sindarov brings**

The numbers make the context stark. Sindarov, who won the 2025 World Cup and the 2026 Candidates, has the best rating performance in the world over the last 12 months at 2812. Gukesh is 39th on that list at 2654.

Carlsen has called Sindarov a "significant favorite," and [Kasparov leans toward Gukesh while Nakamura and Carlsen back Sindarov](/articles/kasparov-leans-gukesh-nakamura-and-carlsen-pick-sindarov/).

Gukesh's counter is experience. "I do have more experience than him. I've played a world championship," he said.

His 2024 match against Ding Liren in Singapore went to the final classical game, so he knows the strain: "Every day you're going to wake up with like butterflies in your stomach."

Anand offered a warning that cuts both ways. "In a world championship, the players will do things that they won't normally do," he said. "You should be ready for very unexpected things to happen."

Gukesh became the youngest undisputed world champion at 18 in December 2024. The match with Sindarov starts in Geneva at 15:00 CET on Tuesday, November 24.
