The 12th Judit Polgar Global Chess Festival [took place at the Hungarian National Gallery in Budapest on Saturday 26 September](https://en.chessbase.com/post/global-chess-festival-2026-report), with Critical Thinking as its theme. Founded in 2015, the festival has long since outgrown the idea of a chess tournament with side attractions. This year's programme ran from 10:00 to 17:30 and treated chess as a tool for testing assumptions and making decisions.

That framing is a deliberate choice. Polgar, the strongest woman player in history, has spent years promoting chess as an educational method, and the festival is where that argument is made in public, to families and schoolchildren as much as to players.

**A growing format**

The scale has changed a great deal. By 2018, events linked to the festival were held at 250 venues in 23 countries, while the main gathering had already settled at the National Gallery. The 2020 edition was online, later ones were hybrid, and the programme has since returned to a larger on-site schedule.

Recent editions have tackled women in chess and science, decision-making, and the relationship between chess and artificial intelligence. On the main stage, chess players faced AI models designed to reproduce aspects of their own playing style, and a session covered Chess Palace and AI in education.

**Polgar at the boards**

The main chess event was the Community Chess Simul on the first floor of the gallery, hosted online by IM Anna Rudolf. Instead of facing individual opponents, Polgar played five communities, whose representatives could draw on ideas and analysis from members following remotely.

She finished with 3½ out of 5: three wins, one draw and one loss. The loss came against the Pietro Chess community, which [we covered separately](/articles/judit-polgar-drops-a-game-to-pietro-s-community-in-budapest/). The Chess Is For Everyone team held her to a draw.

One opponent worked without a crowd. The Hungarian Stars team, a group of intellectuals led by neuroscientist Szilveszter E. Vizi, played without help from an online community. Australian streamer Hannah Sayce, by contrast, played with the backing of her online community.

Polgar also played a blindfold game against Hungarian GM Gergely Antal, which ended in a draw. Her sister Sofia Polgar, who showed the moves of that game to the audience, joined Judit and GM Krisztian Szabo in the Master Relay Simul, where several masters take turns continuing the same games.

**Chess meets the Rubik's Cube**

The RubikChess Tournament was open to the general public for the first time. It was organised with the FIDE Commission for Women's Chess and support from Rubik's, and Ernő Rubik and Sofia Polgar attended the opening.

Teams of two played five rounds. In each, one partner played a chess game while the other solved a 3×3 cube, with the teams choosing beforehand who did which. Chess players were capped at a rating of 1800, so the field was aimed at amateurs and cube enthusiasts.

RubikChess had debuted at the previous festival, and winners of that first competition demonstrated the format on the main stage with commentary from Judit Polgar.

**Why the format works**

The community simul is the clearest example of the festival's thinking. A single master facing a crowd of anonymous opponents is a familiar spectacle, but five boards backed by collective analysis turn the exhibition into a contest between one calibre of judgment and many pooled opinions. It is also a neat fit for a critical-thinking theme: whose advice do you trust, and when?

Other items filled out the day, including Live Chess Painting, the Chess Palace Adventure Trail, robot programming, logic activities, and a screening of the documentary Queen of Chess.
