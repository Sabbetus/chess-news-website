Uzbekistan finished the 46th FIDE Chess Olympiad with 20 match points and 32½ game points, and the open team then sat down with ChessBase's Sagar Shah after the closing ceremony. In that [interview with the gold medalists](https://en.chessbase.com/post/uzbekistan-winners-interview-olympiad-2026), the tone is loose and funny, but the players are specific about what actually decided the event.

The big one, by consensus, was the round six win over India. Yakubboev, who beat Nihal, called it the most important match of the tournament: "It decided the whole standings." He added that if Uzbekistan had lost it, he doubted they would have fought for a medal at all.

Yakubboev's game turned on a blunder. Nihal played 37...Nf5? and lost his queen to a check on e6. Yakubboev said he had spotted the ...Kg7 idea earlier, when Nihal took on g5, and "felt a bit happy." He called ...Kg7 the decisive moment for the team.

The rest of the squad was watching. Nodirbek said everyone went crazy when the 38...Ne6+ check appeared, and that the team chat was screaming. Asked whether they were following privately or doing live commentary, Sindarov answered in one word: "On Lichess."

## Aura, content and a Scandinavian

Sindarov was asked about his queen sacrifice against Peru's Diego Saul Rodri, built around 31...Kf7 and 32...Rh3. He waved off the idea that it was deep. Other moves, he said, were nine or ten moves long, while 31...Kf7 "was just making some aura." He had doubted it during the game, then decided to play "for some videos, for some content."

Nodirbek supplied another aura moment. Against a player he had checked had no practice in the Scandinavian, he answered 1.e4 with 1...d5. He told teammates about twenty minutes before the game, and at first nobody believed him. Yakubboev noted it had been raised in the team meeting, and Nodirbek agreed it was discussed briefly.

The idea was borrowed. Nodirbek said Arjun won a nice game with it in the last round in Budapest, "so I also tried to steal his aura." That is a small, telling detail: the top Uzbek player lifting an opening from an Indian rival, the same team he says Uzbekistan needed to beat.

Nodirbek also went unbeaten on board one, playing every round without a loss. His own account is modest. He says he played very safe at times, won some games, and worked hard beforehand so he would not let the team down.

## Preparation, luck and a lost 40 minutes

Shamsiddin, who held Gukesh with Black, credited memory. He had seen the 11...b5 line one or two months earlier, not on the day of preparation, and knew that after ...b5 equalising is easy. He pointed to wins over the USA, India and China as the matches that mattered.

Mukhiddin described his late win over Gabriel Sargissian. After Black's 27...Qb7?, he played 28.Bb5, then 29.d5 and 30.h5, sensing the king was in trouble. He also admitted the first two rounds were hard because of the pressure.

Sindarov was the most candid about luck. Against Armenia's Robert Hovhannisyan, his preparation lasted until roughly move 20 and only gave equality. Then, he said, the opponent played "a really bad game" and gave up pawns. "Sometimes you need to be lucky to win this tournament."

Against Ivanchuk he spent 40 minutes trying to recall a line he had seen before the event, failed, and played a move of his own that was not good. He still drew. His rule of thumb: when he draws, he knows the team will win the match easily.

## Home pressure and the payday

Nodirbek admitted to feeling a little pressure at home. He contrasted it with India in Chennai, who he said were close but made mistakes at critical moments and could not close it out. He called the event "incomparable with any other event" and said he expects "many more victories to come."

Sagar noted the government reward: each player on the gold-medal team receives US$200,000 and the coach US$50,000, according to a ChessBase India post. Shamsiddin's response was short. The President supports chess, he said, and takes care of them.

Captain Rustam Kasimdzhanov drew the warmest words. Yakubboev said the players grew up watching his games, and some might not be playing chess without him. He also valued having a captain from their own country who knows their character and how they think at the board.

For more on how the team was built, see The Chess Herald's look at [Uzbekistan's gold-medal team](/articles/the-anti-motivator-and-the-silent-killer-inside-uzbekistan-s-gold-medal-team/). Sindarov's closing hope in the interview was simple: to play more tournaments for gold with this same group.
