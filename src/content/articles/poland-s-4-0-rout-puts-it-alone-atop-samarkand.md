Poland no longer has to share the top of the table. Going into the penultimate round of the 3rd FIDE Chess Olympiad for People with Disabilities in Samarkand, [Poland had been tied at the summit with the Czech Republic](/articles/cuba-s-unbeaten-run-ends-as-poland-and-czechia-share-the-lead/).

Day 6 settled that question emphatically: a 4-0 sweep of the very team they were sharing first place with, sending Poland into the final round as the sole leader with nothing left to prove against their closest rival.

That kind of result, a clean shutout against the team right behind you, is about as decisive a way to separate from the pack as chess allows.

Whatever happens on the final board, Poland now controls its own destiny, and the Czech Republic has to hope for help elsewhere in the standings to catch back up. You can read the full breakdown of how the round played out on [FIDE's official recap](https://www.fide.com/olympiad-for-people-with-disabilities-poland-takes-sole-lead-as-clean-sport-and-fair-play-take-spotlight-on-day-6/).

The bigger story of Day 6, though, was what happened away from the boards. For the first time, this event overlapped directly with the main event: competition at the 46th FIDE Chess Olympiad also began in Samarkand on the same day, meaning the disability Olympiad and the Open and Women's Olympiads are, for the first time, sharing a host city simultaneously.

Nigel Short made the ceremonial first move to open Round 6.

That proximity matters more than it might sound. Kenya's Ronny Otieno, who competed at the previous edition in Kazakhstan, said he had already run into stars of the main event, including Vasyl Ivanchuk, Praggnanandhaa Rameshbabu and Gukesh D. For an athlete whose introduction to serious tournament chess came through a college dare from friends, sharing a city with the world's best players is a genuinely new kind of experience.

The other throughline of the day was clean sport, and it marks a real evolution for this competition. Anti-doping controls, not just anti-cheating measures, were part of the event this year, a first for an Olympiad built specifically for players with disabilities.

Classification Commission representative Dorian David was candid about the logistics: "It was a challenge because testing people with disabilities is not easy at all. But I can tell you that there weren't any problems." He also stressed that Therapeutic Use Exemptions exist for players on necessary medication, and that responsibility for what's in an athlete's system ultimately rests with the athlete.

Fair Play Officer Jürgen Klüners, who had worked the Grand Swiss as an arbiter in the same city, described the adjustment required at this event as unlike anything else on the calendar. Checking players "who have several problems," in his words, meant leaning on patience rather than a standard script.

He also pointed to something structural: players here get the same DGT boards, clocks, live results screens and press access as at any major FIDE event.

The event's growth trajectory adds context to all of this. This edition has already drawn a record field, something [previously tracked as the Olympiad hit 40 teams](/articles/samarkand-s-disability-olympiad-hits-a-record-40-teams/), and a delegation from Abu Dhabi was on hand Day 6 scouting logistics ahead of hosting the 2028 edition. Vietnam competed for the first time this year too, with Thuy Thi Bich Tran calling it a milestone for her country's federation.

Anti-doping testing, shared venues with elite grandmasters, a first-time delegation from a future host city: none of that existed at this event a decade ago. What started as a comparatively small competition is now being run with the same infrastructure, and increasingly the same scrutiny, as the sport's biggest stage. One round remains, and Poland will play it knowing the title is theirs to lose.
