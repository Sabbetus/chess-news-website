An Amsterdam court has thrown out Vladimir Kramnik's lawsuit against New In Chess, ruling that the Dutch magazine's article accusing the former world champion of cyberbullying GM Daniel Naroditsky was not unlawful. Kramnik says he will appeal, which means this fight is far from finished.

The ruling itself is a legal footnote. What it represents is bigger. Kramnik has spent years building a public case that dozens of elite players, streamers, and now a major chess publication have wronged him, whether through unproven cheating accusations or unflattering coverage. Losing in court doesn't just cost him a verdict, it hands his critics a concrete, official rebuke to point to the next time he goes after someone.

That matters because Kramnik's pattern has been to escalate rather than retreat. He has repeatedly used blog posts, interviews, and now litigation to press his grievances against players like Naroditsky, whose sudden death last year turned the underlying dispute from an online spat into something far more sensitive. A Dutch judge deciding the magazine acted lawfully in describing Kramnik's conduct as cyberbullying is about as public a rejection of his framing as it gets.

The appeal announcement tells you Kramnik isn't backing down, and that's consistent with everything about how he's handled these conflicts. But appeals in defamation cases rarely just repeat the same arguments louder. If the original ruling turned on the court finding the magazine's characterization defensible, Kramnik's legal team will need a materially different angle to have a shot the second time around, not just a louder version of the same grievance.

There's also a broader question hanging over this for chess media generally. Outlets covering online harassment among top players have had reason to be cautious, given how litigious Kramnik has been. A court explicitly declining to punish a publication for calling his behavior cyberbullying gives other chess journalists more room to report on player conduct without the same fear of legal blowback. That's a meaningful outcome regardless of what happens on appeal.

For Naroditsky's supporters, many of whom have been vocal since his death about the toll public accusations and online pressure can take on players, the ruling reads as a form of vindication for how his situation was covered. For Kramnik, it's one more setback in a years-long campaign that has increasingly put him on the defensive rather than the offensive. How the appeal plays out will say a lot about whether that campaign has any legal runway left, or whether the courts have effectively closed the door on this particular front of his broader dispute with the chess world.
