Bibisara Assaubayeva and Anna Muzychuk have punched their tickets to the 2026 WR Women's Chess Tour Grand Final by winning the tour's European leg in Saint-Tropez, a result that closes the qualifying field for the year. Assaubayeva needed two rounds of armageddon to get there, which is its own kind of drama, but it's Muzychuk's presence in the field that carries the longer story.

Back in 2017, Muzychuk was the reigning Women's World Rapid and Blitz champion and one of the sport's biggest stars, yet she chose not to defend either title at that year's championships in Riyadh. She objected publicly to Saudi Arabia's dress requirements and guardianship rules for women, and she gave up both crowns and the prize money that came with them rather than compete under those conditions. It was a costly stand for a player at the peak of her career, and it forced the chess world to reckon openly with where its biggest women's events were being staged and on whose terms.

Nine years on, the sport's premier women's circuit runs through Saint-Tropez instead of Riyadh, and Muzychuk is qualifying for a Grand Final built on a tour model that didn't exist when she made her stand. The WR Women's Chess Tour is designed to funnel top players through a season of events on neutral, sponsor-backed ground before crowning a champion at a season-ending final, a structure that simply gives players more places to compete and fewer reasons to choose between principle and prize money.

That doesn't mean the underlying tensions Muzychuk raised have vanished from chess as a whole. Host selection for major events remains a live and occasionally uncomfortable question, and plenty of players still weigh political and human rights considerations before accepting invitations. But the fact that a player who once sacrificed world titles over exactly those concerns is now qualifying for a season finale through a French Riviera stop says something about how much leverage the women's circuit has built for itself since 2017.

Assaubayeva's route was noisier in the moment, two armageddons will do that, but Muzychuk's qualification is the one worth sitting with. It's a reminder that boycotts and open letters can actually move an entire calendar, even if it takes the better part of a decade to see where the pieces land. The Grand Final field is now set, and for at least one of its members, just being there is a small vindication.
