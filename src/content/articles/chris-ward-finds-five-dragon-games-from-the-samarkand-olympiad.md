In the Olympiad special of ChessBase's Monthly Dragon series, British GM Chris Ward [picks five Dragon games from the Olympiad in Samarkand](https://en.chessbase.com/post/dragons-of-the-olympiad-2026-the-monthly-dragon). He draws them from a wide range of rating levels. His aim is to show how successful the opening was at the event.

Ward is one of the opening's best-known advocates. He has written several books on it, keeps a blog on chesspublishing, and has made three FritzTrainers on the Dragon for ChessBase.

**The themes Ward highlights**

The five games illustrate typical Dragon ideas. Ward points to pressure on the c file, the strength of the dark-squared bishop, exchange sacrifices, and tactical ideas around the queenside.

He also stresses something less glamorous: knowing the right plans once opening theory ends. In the Dragon that matters a great deal, because the structure is fixed early and the plans follow from it.

**Where White goes wrong**

Ward also looks at several instructive mistakes. His point is how quickly a position can collapse when White loses track of key Dragon motifs.

This is a familiar pattern with the opening. Black's setup, with the bishop on g7 aiming down the long diagonal, rewards players who know where the counterplay comes from. White players who drift, or who treat the position as a generic Sicilian, can find themselves in trouble fast.

**Why it matters at an Olympiad**

The Dragon is a sharp choice in a team event, where one lost game can swing a match. Seeing it work at several rating levels suggests it is not only a weapon for elite specialists.

The full analysis is in the ChessBase video, and the Monthly Dragon archive is on the ChessBase news site. For readers who want the opening in depth, Ward's FritzTrainers are in the ChessBase shop.
