FIDE has [issued a statement](https://www.fide.com/fide-statement-on-the-conclusion-of-the-total-chess-world-championship-tour-pilot-2026/) on the Total Chess World Championship Tour Pilot 2026 in Budapest. It calls Magnus Carlsen's conduct in forfeiting games "unacceptable" and says it will refer them to the FIDE Ethics and Disciplinary Commission (EDC). The statement is signed by president Timur Turlov and deputy president Viswanathan Anand.

It also congratulates the medalists, Alireza Firouzja, Anish Giri and Fabiano Caruana. It thanks Kjell Madland, the Total Chess team and the Hungarian hosts, noting the pilot comes ahead of the Tour's first full season in 2027.

**What FIDE objects to**

FIDE separates two kinds of forfeit. Some, including Carlsen's, came in matches already mathematically decided. These breached the rule that all scheduled games be played, but FIDE says they did not change any match result.

The second kind is the problem.

Carlsen forfeited three games over two days of his match against Vladislav Artemiev while the outcome was still open: two rapid games on Thursday and the fast classical game on Friday.

[Chess.com reports](https://www.chess.com/news/view/fide-carlsen-total-chess-forfeits-ethics-commission) that he then [won the deciding armageddon game](#game-embed). By its count he forfeited five games in the final four days and finished 17th.

FIDE calls this "unprecedented at the highest level of professional chess." It adds that the games seriously affected the top of the rating standings.

Because unplayed games do not change a rating, the forfeits also spared Carlsen the rating risk of playing them. We [covered the two rapid forfeits](/articles/carlsen-forfeits-two-rapid-games-then-wins-and-stays-above-2800/) when they happened, with his 2800 at stake.

**Possible consequences**

If the EDC finds a breach, the options include a warning, a fine or a suspension. Carlsen has been here before. In December 2023 the commission fined him €10,000 for withdrawing from the 2022 Sinquefield Cup after losing to Hans Niemann, and cleared him of three other charges.

**The arbiters push back**

The arbiters are also in the frame. FIDE says the officiating "fell short of fundamental standards," as they "effectively abdicated their responsibility to uphold the FIDE Laws of Chess and tournament regulations." Their actions go to the Arbiters' Commission and its Disciplinary Sub-Committee.

The tournament's chief arbiter, Anastasia Sorokina, answered the same day with an open letter to Turlov and Anand on X, defending the ten arbiters she led.

She says they followed the FIDE Laws of Chess and the regulations FIDE and Norway Chess approved together, that she was in daily contact with the organisers, and that no player appealed an arbiter's decision or filed a formal complaint.

She also says no FIDE representative contacted her during the event or before the statement went out, and that FIDE named no specific breach.

Accusing the arbiters before any proceedings, she argues, prejudges the outcome and ignores the presumption of innocence. She demands that FIDE withdraw the allegation, publish a retraction through the same channels and apologise to the arbiters.

**The players weigh in**

Caruana was blunt about the Artemiev match. "I don't think it's good at all," he told ChessBase India, adding: "I think you should just forfeit the match if you don't play." He compared it to a tennis player dropping the second set and returning for the third.

Ian Nepomniachtchi went further: "I think the player has to be expelled from the tournament." Maxime Vachier-Lagrave said he was sad that some players "decided to make their own headlines," then sharpened it on X, apparently aimed at Carlsen: "Correction: that a crybaby decided to make his own headlines."

Levon Aronian struck a different tone. He asked disappointed fans to see Carlsen's actions "as a blessing for chess" and praised the younger generation's hunger.

**A defense built on the rules**

Wadim Rosenstein, a former FIDE presidential candidate and tournament organizer, defended Carlsen. His argument is about incentives. A player who had already won a match could lose rating points by drawing the last game, while forfeiting cost nothing in rating.

"Why did the tournament regulations create a situation in which not playing was more beneficial than playing?" he asked. Rosenstein also said Carlsen should have made sure Bibisara Assaubayeva, his opponent in Tuesday's forfeited game, was told of his decision.

That point is close to where FIDE itself ends up. It says it will work with Total Chess to refine the forfeit rules, since Budapest was meant as a test. Whether a rule gap explains the Artemiev forfeits, which came while that match was undecided, is what the EDC may have to weigh.
