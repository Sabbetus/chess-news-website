The second dispute to come out of the Total Chess pilot in Budapest is still growing.

FIDE's statement of 21 November accused the event's arbiters of having "effectively abdicated their responsibility to uphold the FIDE Laws of Chess and tournament regulations," and [referred them to its Arbiters' Commission](/articles/fide-sends-carlsen-s-forfeits-to-ethics-commission/).

Chief arbiter Anastasia Sorokina answered the same day with an open letter rejecting the charge and demanding a retraction.

Since then, players and officials have taken her side, and one former FIDE official has turned the criticism back on FIDE's own rules.

**Sorokina's case**

Sorokina led a team of ten arbiters. In her letter to FIDE president Timur Turlov and deputy president Viswanathan Anand, she says they followed the FIDE Laws of Chess and the regulations FIDE and Norway Chess approved together.

She adds that no player appealed an arbiter's decision, that she received no formal complaint, and that no FIDE representative contacted her during the event or before the statement was published.

**Support from players and federations**

Wesley So, the four-time U.S. champion, spoke up on 22 November. BruvsChess Media reported him calling Sorokina "one of the nicest and most attentive arbiters I know, and very professional."

So added his own voice in a reply on X, saying he has known her for years and has had "nothing but positive interactions" with her.

The German Chess Federation backed her too. According to BruvsChess Media, its president Paul Meyer-Dunker posted that he and the federation are "100 % in support of Anastasia and her team" against FIDE's criticism.

**A rulebook argument**

George Mastrokoukos, who worked at FIDE before 2019, framed the dispute differently. He argues that the problem lies in the Total Chess regulations, not with the arbiters or the organisers.

Writing on X, he says FIDE's event rules used to include provisions for exactly the issues raised in Budapest. As examples he cites three articles from FIDE's 2018 World Championship match regulations. One ends a match once a player reaches the winning score, and another lets the chief arbiter fine a player up to 5,000 euros.

A third sets penalties for breaches of the regulations or of sportsmanship.

In his view, the current administration of Arkady Dvorkovich, Turlov and Anand left such articles out of the new Total Chess regulations, "tying both the arbiter's and the organiser's hands."

His argument is close to the one Wadim Rosenstein made in Carlsen's defence: that the regulations, not the people applying them, created the situation. FIDE itself has said it will work with Total Chess to refine the forfeit rules.

**What comes next**

Praggnanandhaa, one of the pilot's players, kept his view private. "I've shared my thoughts with the team privately," he wrote on X, adding that the pilot "is a good way to test the format" and that he hopes it succeeds.

The arbiters' case now sits with FIDE's Arbiters' Commission and its Disciplinary Sub-Committee, while Carlsen's forfeits go to the Ethics and Disciplinary Commission. Sorokina has asked FIDE to withdraw the allegation against her team.
