Game 3 of the 2026 World Chess Championship in Geneva ended in a draw on Thursday, and it was the first game of the match in which [the defending champion clearly won the opening fight](https://en.chessbase.com/post/world-championship-2026-3). Sindarov, White, held after 60 moves. The match stands at 1½-1½ going into today's rest day.

**A deeper Berlin**

Sindarov again began [1.e4](#ply-1), and Gukesh again chose the Berlin Defence. This time he avoided the endgame of [Game 1](/articles/gukesh-holds-sindarov-to-a-38-move-draw-in-game-1-in-geneva/).

After 4.0-0 Nxe4 [5.d4](#ply-9) he played [5...Be7](#ply-10), the Rio de Janeiro Variation. Anish Giri joked that the contenders had already "visited" Berlin, London and Rio.

The real surprise was [8...Nf5](#ply-16). Around 90% of games from that position have gone 8...Nb7, so Gukesh was well off the main road. Sindarov used six and nine minutes on his next two moves while Gukesh kept playing fast.

**Following Spassky**

The moves through [13...Qb8](#ply-26) had been played before, in Ljubojevic vs Spassky at the 1988 World Cup in Belfort. That game was drawn, though Spassky had to defend carefully with Black. It is a neat irony that the champion was repeating a defence once held by a former world champion.

Sindarov's [14.a3](#ply-27) was a novelty, but he was already 40 minutes behind on the clock. Gukesh blitzed out his next two moves, which suggested he had studied the position in depth. The queens were off by move 17, leaving unbalanced pawn structures and active pieces.

**Gukesh presses, Sindarov counters**

Gukesh gained space on the kingside and found several subtle manoeuvres. Sindarov, out-prepared for the first time in the match and well behind on the clock, had an uncomfortable position to defend.

The turning point was [29.f4](#ply-57), a kingside break. Sindarov was critical of it at the press conference. Gukesh said he had overlooked the idea, even though he expected his opponent to seek counterplay on that wing.

Gukesh spent over 15 minutes on his reply, his longest think of the game. Engines still rated the position as equal, but Black's active plans had become harder to find.

Anish Giri suggested on X that 23...g4, with Be6 and Rg8 to follow, was probably a little more precise for Black, and that 29.g4 was probably a better try for White than 29.f4. If anything, ChessBase judged, Sindarov had the slightly more promising position.

**Six seconds at move 40**

The clock became the story. Sindarov had to play roughly a minute per move, yet his accuracy barely dipped. He made his 40th move with only six seconds left.

The game then reached an endgame with rooks and bishops of opposite colours. The players kept going until move 60 before agreeing a draw.

**What it means**

A preparation edge that yields no point is a mixed result for Gukesh. He showed that his team can steer Sindarov into lines he does not know, but the Uzbek challenger absorbed the pressure.

Sindarov said he was "happy that I defended very well." Gukesh described the game as "a lot of thinking, a lot of trying to outsmart the other."

Game 4 is on Saturday 28 November, with the 14-game match scheduled to finish on 11 December. Tiebreaks, if needed, are on 12 December.
