FIDE has [opened ticket sales for the Salesforce FIDE World Championship](https://www.fide.com/ticket-sales-for-the-salesforce-fide-world-championship-are-now-open/), the match between World Champion Gukesh D of India and Challenger Javokhir Sindarov of Uzbekistan, both 20. The event opens on 22 November in Geneva, Switzerland, with the first game scheduled for 24 November.

A world title match is being sold to the public as a live experience rather than only a broadcast. The choices are simple, and the pricing is where the interesting part lies.

**Two ticket types**

The first is the VIP Day Pass, which includes an hour in the playing hall. Because the number of VIP Passes is strictly limited, holders get an exclusive view of the games from the balcony overlooking the playing hall. FIDE pitches this as a chance to experience the hall's palpable silence.

VIP holders also get the VIP Lounge, where live commentary will come from GMs Peter Svidler and Peter Leko. Svidler and Leko are both long-time elite players, so the commentary is a real draw for anyone who wants the games explained as they unfold.

The second is the Fan Zone Pass. The Fan Zone is meant to be a day out in itself: simuls, tournaments, master classes, opportunities to play chess, photo opportunities, and a live broadcast from the playing hall on the big screen. Q&As with grandmasters are offered on selected days.

**Dynamic pricing**

The number of tickets is limited, and they will be released in stages with dynamic pricing. FIDE gives no price figures in its announcement, so what a day pass will cost is something buyers will have to check on the ticketing site. The stated advice is not to wait until the last moment.

Staged release with dynamic pricing is a model familiar from other live events, and it signals that FIDE expects demand to climb as the match nears. It also suggests the organisers want to reward early buyers while capturing more revenue if the match turns tight. That is a reading, not something FIDE says.

**Why the format fits**

The match pairs a reigning champion with a challenger of the same age, and FIDE frames it as a fight for the right to be called the leader of their generation. That narrative is easy to sell to fans, and a small playing hall with a silent balcony is a natural way to monetise the closeness of the contest.

How a player reaches such a match is covered in our [guide to the world championship cycle](/articles/world-championship-cycle/). Tickets are sold at tickets.fide.com.
