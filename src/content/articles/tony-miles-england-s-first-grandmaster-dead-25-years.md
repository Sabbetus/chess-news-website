Twenty-five years ago yesterday, on 12 November 2001, [Anthony "Tony" Miles was found dead in his bed](https://en.chessbase.com/post/anthony-miles-25-years-death). An old school friend had come to collect him for a game of bridge. Miles had died of heart failure, a consequence of long-term diabetes.

He was 46. The diabetes had been diagnosed only three years earlier, and ChessBase notes it had almost certainly been behind many of his episodes of weakness and poor form.

**A prodigy from Birmingham**

Miles was born on 23 April 1955 in a suburb of Birmingham. In 1968 he won the English Under-14 Championship, and he soon became the strongest player in the Midlands.

He was runner-up at the 1973 World Junior Championship and won the title a year later. In 1976 he became the first Englishman awarded the grandmaster title by FIDE. That also earned him the £5,000 prize offered by the patron Jim Slater for the feat.

**The a6 game**

His most famous result came at the 1980 European Team Championship in Skara, where he beat reigning world champion Anatoly Karpov with the unorthodox [1...a6](#ply-2). It was not meant as a provocation. Miles had noticed that Karpov knew all of his usual openings perfectly, and he wanted to pull the champion out of his comfort zone.

It was not his only win over Karpov. Miles was also a committed Sicilian Dragon player, and he used it with regular success through the 1970s and 1980s.

In 1985 he played some of his games in Tilburg lying on a stretcher because of back pain. The picture went around the world. In 1986 he reached ninth in the world rankings, his highest place in the top ten.

**England's golden generation**

With John Nunn, Nigel Short and Jonathan Speelman, Miles helped make the English team one of the strongest in the world. Only the Soviet team was stronger.

His career, though, was repeatedly marked by setbacks for which he never found a satisfactory explanation. With the diagnosis coming so late, the diabetes is a plausible, if unproven, part of that story.

**Dispute and breakdown**

Miles had a caustic sense of humour and said what he thought. In 1987 he fell into a dispute with Raymond Keene and the English Chess Federation over payments he believed were unjustified. His relationship with the rising Nigel Short was also strained.

The conflicts took a heavy toll. He suffered a nervous breakdown, and in September 1987 police detained him in Downing Street, where he was apparently trying to reach Margaret Thatcher to discuss the dispute. He spent a period in psychiatric care.

**Years abroad, then home**

Miles left the English federation and played for the US federation for a time. From 1988 he lived for a while near Cologne. He also spent some time in Australia.

He returned to England in 1991 and got good tournament results again in the mid-1990s. He also served for ten years as president of his Birmingham chess club, where members remembered a friendly, humorous and generous organiser. He gave lessons too, and did not think himself above teaching beginners, children included.

He was still playing in his final year, at Scarborough in 2001, where he faced Lawrence Trent.
