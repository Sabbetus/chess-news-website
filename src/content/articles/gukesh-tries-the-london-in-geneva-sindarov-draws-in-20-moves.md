Game 2 of the 2026 World Chess Championship match in Geneva ended in a quiet draw on Wednesday. [Gukesh Dommaraju and Javokhir Sindarov split the point](https://en.chessbase.com/post/world-championship-2026-2) after a threefold repetition, leaving the score level at 1-1 after two games.

Gukesh had White for the first time and chose the London System: [1.d4](#ply-1) d5 [2.Nf3](#ply-3) Nf6 [3.Bf4](#ply-5). For a player with a reputation for pushing for a win, sometimes to a fault, it was a cautious pick. After [the 38-move draw in Game 1](/articles/gukesh-holds-sindarov-to-a-38-move-draw-in-game-1-in-geneva/), it was also a very different kind of game.

**A defence of the London**

At the post-game press conference Gukesh was upbeat about his choice. "I don't know why it gets so much hate, it's a cool opening," he said.

Commentator Peter Svidler offered a strategic reading. Playing the London lets the champion test Sindarov's preparation without showing the lines he has saved for when he wants to press harder. That is a reasonable way to see it, though Gukesh himself hasn't said as much.

The London has rarely appeared in World Championship matches. Ding Liren is the only player to win a game with it in a title match, beating Ian Nepomniachtchi in game six of their 2023 match in Astana. Ding later used it twice against Gukesh in Singapore in 2024, drawing games six and ten.

By coincidence, the system showed up in Geneva on the day the London Chess Classic began in the English capital, with Nodirbek Abdusattorov and Vasyl Ivanchuk among the players.

**Where the game turned**

As in Game 1, Sindarov moved faster than Gukesh in the opening, though Gukesh was still within his preparation. The first real surprise was [11...Qd8](#ply-22). Peter Leko, a remarkable positional player himself, was impressed by the challenger's approach and the quality of his preparation.

Gukesh spent around 25 minutes before playing 12.0-0. After [15...f5](#ply-30) he went into another long think. By then Sindarov had comfortably reached equality.

The champion used nearly half an hour before choosing [16.f3](#ply-31), aiming for the central push e3-e4. Sindarov's [16...Nf6](#ply-32) discouraged that advance.

Gukesh then signalled he would take the draw with [17.Be5](#ply-33). After [17...Nd7](#ply-34) [18.Bg3](#ply-35), the players repeated the position three times and the game ended after 20 moves, according to ChessBase's headline count. Annotator Stefan Liebig covered the game for ChessBase.

**What it suggests**

Game 1's Berlin Defence offered more tension. Game 2 offered little, and the clock told the story: Sindarov looks either better prepared or more confident, while Gukesh seems content to ease in. Close to an hour spent on two moves is a lot for a game that ended in equality.

That may be the point, if the long-game view holds. A 14-game match is a marathon, and neither player has shown his sharpest weapons yet. Anand's [50-50 call](/articles/anand-calls-gukesh-vs-sindarov-50-50-and-waits-for-first-blood/) still looks fair.

**What comes next**

Game 3 is today, Thursday 26 November, followed by a rest day on Friday. Game 4 is Saturday 28 November, with Games 5 and 6 on 29 and 30 November. The match runs through Game 14 on 11 December, with tiebreaks, if needed, and the closing ceremony on 12 December.

Gukesh's second this time is Grzegorz Gajewski, who also helped him prepare for the 2024 match in Singapore. Sindarov is working with Nikita Vitiugov.
