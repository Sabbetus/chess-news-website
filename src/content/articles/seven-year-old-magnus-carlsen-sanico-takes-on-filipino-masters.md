Bernard Sanico had settled the question of a baby name long before his son arrived in 2019. A fan of the Norwegian world champion since he was 12, he [named the boy Magnus Carlsen Sanico](https://www.chess.com/news/view/magnus-carlsen-sanico-7-year-old-grandmaster-dream).

Seven years on, the younger Magnus is a rising player in the Philippines, and he has just finished the Misamis Occidental Philippines International Chess Championship, which concluded on November 16.

**A deal before the birth**

The naming was by agreement. "We made a deal. If the baby is a boy, I'll decide the name. If it's a girl, the mother decides," Bernard told Chess.com.

His own reason was simple: "I am the number one fan of the greatest Magnus Carlsen."

The family had no objection, though the choice sometimes needed explaining. His son has known who he was named after since daycare.

**From beginner classes to a first start**

Bernard is 29 and teaches beginners in their hometown of Kolambugan, in Lanao del Norte on Mindanao. He plays only online, where he is rated around 2000. He used to bring Magnus along to those lessons.

In March 2025, aged five, Magnus asked to learn. "Dad, teach me also chess," Bernard recalls him saying. Bernard stresses the interest was the boy's own, and that carrying the name never meant he had to play.

Bernard estimates his son has since played around 35 tournaments, mostly local with some national events. "He puts extra pressure on his opponents because of the name," he joked.

**Two events in November**

At the ASEAN Youth U10 Boys Championship in Misamis Occidental on November 7-9, Magnus scored 4.5/7 and finished 10th. He was playing older children in the under-10 section.

Then came the bigger test: a field that included grandmasters and international masters. Magnus finished on 4/9 with three wins and two draws, which is a strong return for a seven-year-old in that company.

Among those results, he held a 1930-rated opponent to a draw and beat players rated 1516 and 1784. His father says he enjoyed it and was excited to face strong, experienced opponents.

**Ratings and the rest**

Magnus already has published FIDE ratings of 1495 in rapid and 1755 in blitz. A classical rating in the high 1600s is expected on the next list.

That would make him one of the world's highest-rated players born in 2019 or later. For readers new to how these numbers behave, our explainer on [what a chess rating actually measures](/articles/chess-ratings/) covers why juniors' ratings can move so quickly.

The blitz figure is the more striking of the two. A published 1755 at seven suggests a child who handles speed well, though a handful of events is a small sample and early ratings can swing.

**The cost of backing him**

Support has come at a price. Bernard gave up a steady job away from home to focus on his son, and now earns money helping in his uncle's furniture business. The two train daily, but Bernard says his own knowledge is limited and he cannot afford a coach.

Travel is a real expense. Magnus' elementary school and a community official helped pay for the trip to Misamis Occidental, two hours from home, and a business sponsored the family's accommodation at the resort. A Facebook page about Magnus has nearly 7000 followers.

Away from the board, Magnus enjoys school, is good at math and likes hiking. His chess ambition is plain: more tournaments, and the grandmaster title.

Asked about his own dream, Bernard said: "My dream is what Magnus dreams for himself. I just support him."
