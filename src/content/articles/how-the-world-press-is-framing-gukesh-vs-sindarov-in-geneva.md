The World Chess Championship match between Gukesh Dommaraju and Javokhir Sindarov in Geneva has drawn attention well beyond the chess press. ChessBase [collected the coverage from major international outlets](https://en.chessbase.com/post/world-championship-2026-press-review), and the selection shows which stories editors think a general reader will care about.

Three themes recur: the youth of both players, the sharp contrast in their last twelve months, and the psychological weight of a title match. None of them is about opening theory, which is itself telling.

**Form against experience**

The dominant frame is a reigning champion in difficulty facing a challenger on the rise. India Today titled its preview "Gukesh's rocky reign meets Sindarov's remarkable rise," while the Hindustan Times went with "Fortitude meets form."

The Financial Times took the betting angle, reporting Sindarov as odds-on to win in Geneva. Several observers, according to the ChessBase round-up, favour the challenger on the strength of recent results.

The counterweight is that Gukesh has already played a World Championship match and Sindarov has not. Experience of the format is hard to price, and it may be the main reason the "obvious" favourite is less obvious than the odds suggest. For more on how the match is built, see our look at [the prize fund and match format](/articles/gukesh-sindarov-game-1-2-5m-prize-fund-and-match-format/).

**What the players said on Sunday**

At Sunday's press conference and opening ceremony, both players were interviewed by FIDE's Charlyze van Zyl. The Guardian reported Gukesh vowing to win the title "all over again," and ChessBase says several publications picked up his intention to treat the match as a chance to win the title anew rather than as a defence.

Uzbek outlet Kun.uz led with Sindarov's own assessment: he rates his chances against Gukesh at 50-50. That is a measured line from a player many outsiders are backing, and it matches the tone of [Anand's 50-50 reading of the match](/articles/anand-calls-gukesh-vs-sindarov-50-50-and-waits-for-first-blood/).

The Times of India and ChessBase India ran fuller accounts of who said what. Al Jazeera's preview pitched the event as the youngest World Chess Championship in history.

**Beyond the board**

The more distinctive pieces step away from the sporting contest. Bryan Armen Graham's Guardian interview with Sindarov, headlined "'I can't play games just for fun'," portrays a 20-year-old "happy warrior" and explores his competitive attitude even toward his hobbies.

Amit Kamath of The Indian Express looked at the venue, the Fondation Martin Bodmer, and the extraordinary collection of books surrounding the players. A match played among literary classics is an easy image for a features desk, and a rare one for chess.

Canadian writer Stephen Marche, in a Guardian opinion piece, uses the championship as a starting point to ask what human intellectual competition is worth in the age of artificial intelligence. Its headline promises hope, not alarm.

**What the coverage leaves open**

Almost all of this was written before a single game was played. Previews reward narrative, and the narrative here is neat: a struggling champion, a surging challenger. Once the board takes over, the story may turn out to be less tidy.

Game 1, with Sindarov on white, [ended in a 38-move draw](/articles/gukesh-holds-sindarov-to-a-38-move-draw-in-game-1-in-geneva/), and the coming games will show whether the press consensus is reading the match correctly.
