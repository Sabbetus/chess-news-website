FIDE will hold its first dedicated Summit on Chess in Education on 24 September 2026, timed to run alongside the 46th FIDE Chess Olympiad and Congress in Samarkand, Uzbekistan. The event, held at Silk Road by Minyoun and [themed "Every Lesson Counts,"](https://www.fide.com/every-lesson-counts-fide-summit-on-chess-in-education-comes-to-samarkand-this-september/) is billed as a centrepiece of what FIDE has designated the 2026 Year of Chess in Education, and registration is already open for educators, policymakers and researchers who want a seat in the room.

The day is structured in three distinct blocks rather than a single keynote parade. After an opening session hosted by Simon Bélanger, a videographer, actor and chess content creator known online as @simonplayschess, the programme moves into Part II from 14:00 to 15:30: a run of shorter panel discussions covering established national programmes, the growing pains of newer pilot initiatives, and how universities and nonprofits are starting to plug into the space.

Part III, running 16:00 to 18:00 after a coffee break, is where the real substance sits. Six longer Education Innovation Lectures each take on a single theme: chess as a tool for refugee and conflict-affected communities, the history of FIDE's own education programming, how to make chess genuinely enjoyable for kids rather than just instructive, how the game builds learning and community ties worldwide, the results of a new Chess in Education Global Survey, and chess's reach beyond the tournament hall entirely. That's a lot of ground for a single afternoon, and the survey results in particular are the kind of data point that could shape funding conversations well past September.

Practically, FIDE is treating this as a genuinely international gathering rather than a side room at the Olympiad. Real-time voice translation will run in 64 languages, though attendees need to bring their own headphones, and anyone needing an official invitation letter for visa purposes can contact Rita Atkins, Secretary of the FIDE Chess in Education Commission.

There's a useful parallel here to how FIDE has been restructuring its own institutions in recent years. The federation's move to stand up an [Athletes Commission modeled on the IOC's own playbook](/articles/fide-s-new-athletes-commission-echoes-the-ioc-s-playbook/) was a bet that borrowing the format of an older, larger sporting body would give a newer initiative instant credibility. Declaring a "Year of Chess in Education" and giving it a standalone summit, complete with its own Commission and secretary, follows the same logic: international federations from the IOC to UNESCO have long used themed years and dedicated commissions to turn a scattered set of grassroots efforts into something that looks, and functions, like a coordinated movement.

Whether that structure actually accelerates chess-in-education programmes on the ground, as opposed to just organizing the conversation about them, is the open question the Samarkand summit will start to answer. FIDE's own framing leans hard into the ambition: "Every lesson counts. Every child can benefit." The six innovation lectures, especially the one built around the new Global Survey data, are where that claim will get its first real test.

Registration for the Summit is open now at edu-summit2026.fide.com, with the full schedule available as a PDF through FIDE's website.
