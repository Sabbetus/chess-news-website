ChessBase Magazine issue 233 is built around two summer events, the Biel Chess Festival and Norway Chess. In his [review for ChessBase](https://en.chessbase.com/post/cbm-233-review-nagesh-havanur), Nagesh Havanur describes a database of 2911 games, 18 of them deeply annotated, plus ten opening surveys, three opening videos and training material.

The extra draw is Anish Giri's analysis of two decisive games from the Quantbox Chennai Grandmasters tournament. Other annotators include Lubomir Ftacnik, Aydin Suleymanli and Le Quang Liem.

**Aronian takes the Biel triathlon**

The cover star is Levon Aronian, who won the strongest-player triathlon at Biel. Havanur notes that Biel is a festival with an edition for every level, from professionals to amateurs.

Aronian is now in his mid-forties, and Havanur calls him a genial grandmaster with a sunny temperament who, unlike more ambitious players, does not try to win every tournament. At the prize ceremony he said, laughing, "I did not dream of winning against these young monsters."

That relaxed attitude, Havanur argues, paid off. Aronian took his chances when they appeared, and sometimes took crazy risks, winning even from lost positions. The issue includes one such game.

He also kept experimenting in the opening, leaving opponents on their own. One example is a rare French Defence line against Bluebaum, and Havanur thinks Black could answer it with a principled continuation.

**The players behind him get space**

Havanur praises the issue for not ignoring the chasers. Aydin Suleymanli finished second and annotates his own game against Le Quang Liem.

Le Quang Liem had won this Biel tournament three years running before. This time he shared third with Yagiz Erdogmus, and the issue includes a game annotated by the winner himself.

**Norway: Carlsen's slump, Pragg's surge**

The Norway Chess report, Havanur says, is brief and leans toward Magnus Carlsen's poor form. Carlsen lost to Alireza Firouzja, Wesley So and Praggnanandhaa Rameshbaby, and Giri annotates one of those losses.

Havanur adds a fairness point: Carlsen did not play as badly as the table suggests, and the issue also carries his win over the same opponent. It is a useful corrective to a final standing that flattens a messy event.

The decisive story, though, was Praggnanandhaa. He won game after game after six rounds and two consecutive losses, according to the review.

Carlsen was generous about him in an interview with The Hindu on 11 September 2026: "I am a fan. I love watching him. He plays in a very aggressive way. He manages to put pressure on basically everybody, including myself."

**Openings and endgames**

Of the ten opening surveys, Havanur singles out two. Sergey Grigoriants covers the Alekhine-Chatard Attack (C13-C14), where he says Black can try a sideline that may solve his problems.

Balazs Csonka looks at the Marshall Gambit against the Semi-Slav (D31). The review shows one encounter from this year's Candidates Tournament.

The three opening videos are Ivan Sokolov on the Saemisch Variation of the Nimzo-Indian (E24), Danny King on the English Defence (A40) and Mihail Marin on the Exchange Variation of the Grünfeld (D85).

On the endgame side, Karsten Müller offers a video on queen versus two minor pieces. Dorian Rogozenco continues his fortress series with opposite-coloured bishops where one side has two pawns.

A "Readers write" column carries contributions from Zoran Petronijevic, Chris Edwards, Ingo Althoefer and Markus Hochgraefe. Havanur also singles out Alvar Alonso Rosell, whose annotations he finds deep and detailed, full of insight.
