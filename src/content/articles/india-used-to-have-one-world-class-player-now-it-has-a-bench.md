For most of the last three decades, Indian chess meant one name. Viswanathan Anand carried the country's ambitions almost single-handedly from the early 1990s through his world championship reign, and for a long time there was no obvious successor waiting behind him. That gap is the reason this moment feels different.

Anand telling the Times of India that he'd currently rank Praggnanandhaa as the world's best player isn't just a compliment from a legend to an heir. It's Anand, of all people, admitting that the torch has actual weight behind it now, not just one candidate but a cluster: Gukesh already a world champion, Pragg fresh off Norway Chess and the Grand Chess Tour, Arjun Erigaisi hovering near the elite, and a supporting cast deep enough to make Olympiad selection genuinely painful.

That's the historical shift worth sitting with. The Soviet Union built this kind of depth through decades of state-funded chess schools, producing multiple world championship contenders in the same generation almost by design. India built its current depth differently, off the back of one man's fame, a wave of strong coaches and academies that sprang up in his shadow, and a generation of kids who grew up assuming a world champion could look like them. Anand didn't get that head start. He had to be the whole pipeline.

The interview's mention of Sindarov also matters here. Uzbekistan producing a teenager capable of rattling Gukesh is a reminder that India isn't the only country cashing in on a youth boom right now, Central Asia and the broader post-Soviet chess infrastructure are still churning out talent too. India's advantage isn't that it's alone at the top. It's that it now has enough depth to field a team where leaving a player of Pragg's caliber off the Olympiad roster is a real dilemma rather than a formality.

There's a version of this story from thirty years ago where a single Indian grandmaster's rise was treated as a minor miracle, proof the country could compete at all. Now the question isn't whether India can produce a world-class player, it's which world-class player gets the board one slot. That's a different kind of pressure, and a much better problem to have than the one Anand spent most of his career solving alone.

Whether Pragg is actually the number one player in the world right now is a ratings argument for another day. What Anand's comment really signals is that Indian chess has moved from dependency to abundance, and it happened inside one generation.
