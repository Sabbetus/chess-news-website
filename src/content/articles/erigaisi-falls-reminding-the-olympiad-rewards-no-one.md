Round one of the 46th Chess Olympiad in Samarkand went mostly the way the seedings said it should. Favorites collected their match points, top boards behaved, and the standings looked orderly by the end of the day. Then the individual results got read out.

The headline shock came on a board that nobody outside the Thai federation was watching closely. [Thai IM Prin Laohawirapap's win against Indian super-GM Arjun Erigaisi](https://www.chess.com/news/view/2026-samarkand-chess-olympiad-round-1) was picked as the event's Game of the Day, a modest-rated player toppling a super-GM ranked as India's number two.

Elsewhere on the lower boards, Iraqi CM Ali Ehsan Aryan produced another of the day's shocking individual upsets. Two federations that rarely make Olympiad headlines both got them on the same afternoon, while the team results above them stayed largely predictable.

That contrast is really the story of round one, and it is also the oldest story the Olympiad tells. The event is scored by match points between teams, not by any single player's result, so a star losing on board one doesn't automatically cost the match. It just means the other three or four boards have to cover for him, and in round one, apparently, they mostly did.

It's worth remembering how deep India's bench has become before assuming Erigaisi's loss says anything alarming about the team. The Chess Herald has [covered how India went from fielding one truly elite player to fielding a full roster of them](/articles/india-used-to-have-one-world-class-player-now-it-has-a-bench/), which is precisely why one board losing to an IM in round one barely dents the team's overall picture.

A federation with a genuine bench can absorb an off day from its number two in a way a one-star roster never could.

None of that makes Laohawirapap's win less real. Beating a super-GM at an Olympiad, on any board, in any round, is the kind of result a player remembers for the rest of his career, whatever happens to the team score around it.

Chess Olympiads have always produced these moments, a modest name catching a star cold on the day it counts, and it's part of why the event stays compelling even once the top-board results settle into form.

Samarkand's edition has already drawn scrutiny beyond the boards themselves. Viswanathan Anand has spoken about organizational strain even as this Olympiad set attendance records, a tension The Chess Herald examined in its piece on [the cracks Anand flagged behind Samarkand's record-breaking field](/articles/anand-admits-the-cracks-behind-samarkand-s-record-olympiad/).

Round one's individual upsets are a smaller, friendlier kind of chaos by comparison, the sort that makes for a good Game of the Day rather than a headache for organizers.

Erigaisi and India will get chances to respond as the rounds continue, and a single loss in round one rarely defines an Olympiad campaign. What it does confirm, again, is that on the lower boards of a 400-team field, rating gaps are no guarantee of anything.
