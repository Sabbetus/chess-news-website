The 2026 World Chess Championship between defending champion Gukesh Dommaraju and challenger Javokhir Sindarov is being played in Switzerland, and [ChessBase has put together an overview of the country's major chess events](https://en.chessbase.com/post/switzerland-rich-chess-tournament-history).

It runs from two world title finals to a Candidates Tournament that is still counted among the great events in chess history. Here is what stands out, and why it makes a fitting backdrop for the current match.

**Two finals, two very different endings**

In Lausanne in 1998, Anatoly Karpov and Viswanathan Anand met for the FIDE title at a time when the chess world was split. Both had come through a knockout event in Groningen. The match stood 3-3 after six games, with two wins each and two draws, and Karpov took the title with two victories in the tiebreaks.

Garry Kasparov, the reigning world champion, had declined his seeded place in the semifinal because he disliked the controversial format.

Brissago, a small town in Ticino, hosted the 2004 final between Vladimir Kramnik and Peter Leko. Leko led going into the last game, but Kramnik won it to level the match at 7-7. As defending champion, he kept the title because the match was drawn.

That draw-retains rule is worth noting next to today's match. A tied result in Brissago left the champion in place, and Leko, who had led until the final game, left without the crown.

Two years later Kramnik and Veselin Topalov played the reunification match. In 2012 Kramnik also played Levon Aronian in Zurich, a match that ended in a draw.

**Zurich 1953 and the oldest club in the world**

The 1953 Candidates Tournament in Zurich was a 16-player double round-robin, so each player had to play 30 games. Vassily Smyslov won with 18 points, two clear of David Bronstein, Paul Keres and Samuel Reshevsky, who finished level. The winner earned the right to challenge Mikhail Botvinnik.

Bronstein had failed to take the title from Botvinnik in the previous World Championship match, and his tournament book on Zurich is regarded as a classic of chess literature. For anyone curious about how the modern [Candidates Tournament](/events/candidates-tournament/#how-it-works) works, the 30-round marathon is a useful contrast with today's shorter format.

The Zurich Chess Club, founded in 1809 and described as the oldest chess club in the world, marked its anniversaries with strong events. In 1934, for its 125th, Alexander Alekhine won ahead of Max Euwe and Salo Flohr. For the 150th in 1959, Mikhail Tal won ahead of Svetozar Gligoric and 16-year-old Bobby Fischer.

The 200th anniversary in 2009 was an eight-player rapid round-robin. Kramnik finished ahead of Anand and Topalov.

**Biel, Lucerne and the Olympiads**

Biel's tradition began in 1976 with an Interzonal, held for the Biel Chess Club's 75th anniversary. Bent Larsen won half a point ahead of Tigran Petrosian, Lajos Portisch and Mikhail Tal. Robert Hübner was another half-point back, sharing fifth to seventh.

More Interzonals followed in 1985 and 1993, won by Rafael Vaganian and Boris Gelfand. Master tournaments had been played there since 1968, and grandmaster tournaments since 1977. Since 2019 the grandmaster event has been a triathlon of classical, blitz and rapid, and Levon Aronian won it in 2026.

Switzerland has also hosted the [Chess Olympiad](/events/chess-olympiad/#how-it-works) twice. Lugano staged the 18th in 1968, won by the Soviet Union ahead of Yugoslavia and the United States, with 50 other teams. In 1982 Lucerne drew 93 teams in the open section and 45 in the women's, a participation record at the time, and Soviet teams dominated both sections.

Lucerne then hosted the first World Team Championship in 1985, and the next four editions. The Soviet Union won in 1985 and 1989, the United States in 1993 and Russia in 1997, before the event moved to Yerevan in 1999.

**What Geneva inherits**

The Swiss record is mostly one of neutral ground, hosting events in a divided chess world. Lausanne and Brissago both sat in the middle of the title split, and Brissago in particular is seen as a step toward reconciliation.

Geneva's match is a straightforward champion-versus-challenger contest.
