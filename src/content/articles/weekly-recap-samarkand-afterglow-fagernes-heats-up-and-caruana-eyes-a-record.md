The Samarkand Olympiad kept echoing through our 21 stories this week, from short games to German medals. Alongside it came a Swiss in Norway, a US Championship preview, regional tournament roundups and a run of explainers.

**Samarkand, looking back**

Jon Speelman went looking for the Olympiad's shortest decisive games, and [his column on 25-move miniatures](/articles/speelman-hunts-samarkand-s-miniatures-40-games-in-25-moves/) opens with Igor Kovalenko's 15.Nb1!, which trapped a queen in round one. Michael Adams supplied a different kind of surprise when [he met the Sicilian with 3.Bc4 and beat Etienne Bacrot](/articles/adams-plays-3-bc4-against-the-sicilian-and-nobody-laughs/). England and France drew 2-2 that round.

Germany's first men's team medal in 26 years is the subject of [a feature tracing the bronze to Bernd Vökler's youth project in Thuringia](/articles/germany-s-bronze-traces-back-to-bernd-v-kler-s-chess-princes/). Frederik Svane, 22, is part of that story. [His second Olympiad gold](/articles/svane-s-second-olympiad-gold-and-germany-s-board-prize-history/) puts him next to Robert Hübner as Germany's only two-time individual gold winner.

**Fagernes and the Sahel**

In Norway, [six players shared the lead after two rounds in Fagernes](/articles/six-players-share-the-lead-after-two-rounds-in-fagernes/). By round seven, [14-year-old IM Christian Gloeckler led on 6/7](/articles/gloeckler-14-leads-fagernes-on-6-7-with-three-rounds-left/) after beating Lukas Dotzer. Rinat Jumabayev sits half a point back.

Some of the Olympiad's titled players are already looking ahead. [A group is heading to Monastir for the Sahel Chess Festival](/articles/monastir-hosts-africa-s-rare-open-with-sousse-1967-next/), which runs October 24 to November 1 and expects about 300 international competitors.

**Online and the money side**

Hikaru Nakamura reached 3500 in Chess.com blitz, but [Vladislav Artemiev beat him in the final round of Titled Tuesday](/articles/artemiev-beats-nakamura-in-the-final-round-of-titled-tuesday/) and took the title. Nakamura streamed from Sithonia, Greece. Another online oddity is on the way: [Josh Smith, an 1800-rated club player, will face Daniil Dubov in 100 bullet games](/articles/dubov-needs-100-wins-a-club-player-needs-just-half-a-point/) with the handicap built into the payout.

Away from the board, a ChessBase feature reports that [Evren Ucok has spent over $5 million on chess in three years](/articles/evren-ucok-s-5-million-bet-on-young-chess-prodigies/), mostly on very young players. Its author helps run the programme. In Kazakhstan, [President Tokayev received new FIDE President Timur Turlov](/articles/tokayev-receives-turlov-and-kazakhstan-becomes-the-model/) and congratulated him on his election.

**US Championship and a lesson from Larsen**

Fabiano Caruana has won four straight US titles. In Saint Louis, [a fifth would match Samuel Reshevsky's run of five between 1936 and 1942](/articles/caruana-chases-a-fifth-straight-us-title-and-reshevsky-s-record/).

For study, a Master Class lesson shows how [Bent Larsen outplayed Gheorghiu at Monte Carlo 1968](/articles/larsen-s-monte-carlo-1968-win-over-gheorghiu-move-by-move/). It argues his real strength was imbalanced positions, not 1.b3.

**September's tournament maps**

Our monthly roundups covered three continents. In North America, [a 165-player Toronto rapid was the biggest event we tracked](/articles/toronto-s-165-player-rapid-tops-north-america-s-september/), well ahead of a next-best field of 104. In South America, [Cascavel, Araguari and Guayaquil anchored the classical calendar](/articles/cascavel-araguari-and-guayaquil-anchor-south-america-s-september/), with Brazil supplying most of the larger opens and Ecuador the biggest field.

Africa logged 216 events in September. [A rapid in Cairo drew 381 players](/articles/adly-power-play-rapid-draws-381-as-cairo-hosts-africa-s-biggest-field/), the largest single field on the continent's calendar.

**Explainers**

This week's primers cover the basics. One lays out [how a player becomes a grandmaster](/articles/chess-titles/), a title FIDE awards for life. Another covers [how classical, rapid and blitz time controls work](/articles/time-controls/), each with its own rating.

Two more cover the rules of play. [Swiss pairings and tiebreaks](/articles/swiss-system-tiebreaks/) are explained, including the system's first use in Zürich in 1895. [Chess960 keeps every rule but shuffles the back rank](/articles/chess960-freestyle/) before each game. A final guide looks at [how chess fights cheating, online and over the board](/articles/anti-cheating/), where the two settings pose different problems.
