The world champion doesn't pick a challenger. The challenger has to come through a two-year qualifying cycle run by FIDE, ending at the [Candidates Tournament](/events/candidates-tournament/), where eight players compete for one place in the title match.

There are four ways into the Candidates. The first is the [World Cup](/events/chess-world-cup/), a knockout of more than 200 players, where the top three qualify. In 2025 those were Javokhir Sindarov, Wei Yi and Andrey Esipenko.

The second is the [Grand Swiss](/events/fide-grand-swiss/), an 11-round Swiss of about 116 strong players. Its top two qualify. In 2025 that was Anish Giri and Matthias Blübaum.

The third is the FIDE Circuit, a season-long ranking that scores each player's best results in strong classical events. The winner of each year's Circuit qualifies, which means two places per cycle. Praggnanandhaa took the 2025 Circuit.

The last place goes to the highest-rated player not already qualified, provided they have played enough rated games. It rewards consistency rather than one big tournament.

The Candidates itself is a double round-robin over 14 rounds, and only first place counts. The winner plays the reigning champion in the [World Championship](/events/world-chess-championship/) match: up to 14 classical games, first to 7.5 points.

In 2026 that is Gukesh Dommaraju against Sindarov, in Cologny, Switzerland, from 24 November. Sindarov's route shows how the cycle works in practice: a World Cup win at 19 as the 16th seed, then the Candidates.

Women have a parallel cycle with the same shape: a [Women's World Cup](/events/womens-world-cup/), a Women's Grand Swiss, a [Women's Candidates](/events/womens-candidates-tournament/), and a [Women's World Championship](/events/womens-world-chess-championship/) match.
